Research / Prior work

Eddy-current
detumbling

A time-varying magnetic field induces eddy currents in a conductive target; the resulting Lorentz forces produce a torque opposing the target’s rotation relative to the field. Arago V2 builds on the rotating-dipole method developed by Abbott, Hermans and colleagues at the University of Utah (2021–2025) and extends it to a five-axis positioning arm and a gimballed three-axis target.

Detumbler arm v2: design, loads and simulation report

Covers the five-axis arm CAD (13 GLB components), actuator selection, static load and interference checks, a costed parts list with fabrication schedule, and MuJoCo simulations of the gimballed aluminum target. Selected 180-second runs reach the 1°/s stability threshold in roughly 20–39 s, against a magnet-off bearing-friction baseline of about 60 s.

Open issues: stop times do not yet isolate magnetic braking from bearing friction; overlap counts of 1,332 and 165 samples include gimbal and arm-link/hull contacts, not only the stand; magnet mass is 574.1 g by catalog versus 491.62 g in CAD; HJ2 actuator bolt patterns are unconfirmed; two-camera fusion and the EtherCAT adapter are incomplete.

02 / Material scope

Target material
requirements

In the low-frequency regime, eddy-current torque on a conductor scales approximately with its electrical conductivity σ, the field’s rotation rate relative to the target and the square of the local field strength, and depends strongly on conductor geometry: currents need closed paths, so wall thickness, continuity and size relative to standoff distance all matter. A dipole field falls off as 1/r³, so standoff dominates achievable torque. [1] [3]

Representative room-temperature conductivities and expected coupling
MaterialCoupling
Aluminum alloys & copperPrimary target class. Copper ≈ 5.96 × 10⁷ S/m; pure aluminum ≈ 3.8 × 10⁷ S/m; 6061-T6 ≈ 2.5 × 10⁷ S/m. Used in all three cited experimental programs; most spacecraft primary structure, panels and upper-stage tanks are aluminum alloys.
Titanium, stainless steel & CFRPWeak. Titanium alloys ≈ 0.6–2.4 × 10⁶ S/m and 304 stainless ≈ 1.4 × 10⁶ S/m, one to two orders below aluminum. CFRP conducts mainly along its fibers (≈ 10⁴–10⁵ S/m in-plane), giving weak, direction-dependent coupling. Ferritic and martensitic stainless grades are ferromagnetic and add forces outside the eddy-current model.
Glass, polymers, solar cells & MLINegligible. Dielectric substrates carry no eddy currents. Multilayer insulation uses vapor-deposited aluminum films around 100 nm thick, typically perforated and segmented, so induced currents are expected to be small.
Mixed-material objectsDominated by the largest continuous conductive structures. Requires a per-target model of conductor geometry, continuity and mass distribution; a sphere or plate result does not qualify a whole object.

Metal conductivity rises as temperature falls, so on-orbit coupling varies with the target’s thermal state. Values are representative handbook figures, not measurements of specific objects.

03 / Validation

Test
sequence

Ground testing runs in four stages: (1) confirm actuator interfaces, magnet mass and recalculated loads; (2) build and balance the gimbal and measure free-spin decay, gravity bias and repeatability before introducing the magnet; (3) complete two-camera fusion and the EtherCAT interface, then test occlusion and whole-arm clearance across the gimbal envelope; (4) run matched magnet-off, static-field and rotating-field trials, measuring torque, temperature and parasitic coupling with repeat runs and uncertainty.

Stages 2 and 4 address the open questions in prior work: how much of the one-axis braking reported by Allen et al. [2] carries over to a three-axis gimbal, and whether wrench models fitted to slowly moving targets [3] hold for tumbling ones. Detumbling addresses rotation only; capture and deorbit are separate capabilities.
